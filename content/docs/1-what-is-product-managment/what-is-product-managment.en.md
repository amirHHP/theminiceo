## Introduction: why product management is the beating heart of modern organizations

In technology, where competition is ruthless and user needs change at the speed of light, why do some products (such as Slack or Spotify) become daily habits, while others, despite large investment, end up in the startup graveyard? The answer is usually not in the quality of the code or in visual beauty; it lies in product management.

Product management is no longer a side role; it is a vital function that keeps a business alive in a digital market. If you intend to enter this field, or you want to build your organization’s product structure, you need to know exactly what this role is, what it does, and why it is necessary. In this part of the docs, we peel back the layers of product management so we can see its core.

---

## What is product management, exactly?

Product management is the process of strategically guiding every stage of the product lifecycle: from researching and discovering an idea through development, launch, optimization, and even product retirement. That dry, classic definition does not quite do it justice.

The best visual definition of product management is the Venn diagram first introduced by *Martin Eriksson*. On that definition, product management sits at the intersection of three main domains:

### 1. Business
Product management is essentially a commercial duty whose goal is to maximize the product’s value for the business. That includes reaching strategic goals, return on investment (ROI), and a sustainable revenue model. The product manager must understand the language of executives, sales, and marketing, and make sure the product is moving toward profitability.

### 2. Technology
The product manager does not need to be a senior programmer, but they must have a deep grasp of how the product is built. They must know technical constraints, understand how complex it is to implement features, and be able to work constructively with the engineering team so decisions stay realistic.

### 3. User experience (UX)
This may be the most important circle in the diagram. The product manager is the user’s voice inside the organization. With deep empathy, they must identify the user’s needs, pains, and motives, and make sure the product that gets built not only works, but is enjoyable and simple to use.

> **Key point:** The product manager is the person who balances these three domains. They decide *what* should be built and *why* it should be built, while the engineering team decides *how* to build it and the design team specifies *how it looks and feels*.

## The product manager role: product CEO or servant leader?

There is an old, and somewhat dangerous, cliché that says: *“The product manager is the CEO of the product.”*
This sentence is true in some ways, because the product manager holds final responsibility for the product’s success or failure. But it is wrong in one very important way: **the product manager has no direct executive power over the teams.**

Product managers cannot order engineers what code to write or tell designers which color to choose. Instead, they must lead through **influence without authority**.

### Key traits of the product manager role:
* **Data-informed decision-maker:** They set personal opinions aside and decide based on quantitative data (analytics) and qualitative data (user interviews).
* **Translator of different languages:** They translate and connect between the engineering team (which speaks code), the business team (which speaks dollars), and the design team (which speaks experience).
* **Ruthless prioritizer:** Resources are always limited. The product manager’s main role is to say “no” to hundreds of good ideas so they can say yes to a few *great* ones.

## Core duties and responsibilities (from strategy to delivery)

A product manager’s duties vary with company size and product type, but they generally fall into three main groups:

### 1. Product discovery
Before writing even one line of code, the product manager must be sure they are solving the right problem.
* **Market research:** Reviewing competitors and market trends.
* **User interviews:** Understanding pain points.
* **Defining hypotheses:** Creating an MVP to test ideas quickly.

### 2. Definition and planning
Once the problem is confirmed, the solution must be defined.
* **Setting strategy and vision:** Where will the product be in two years?
* **Roadmap:** Time planning for delivering value.
* **Backlog grooming:** Turning large goals into executable user stories.

### 3. Delivery and optimization
Working with the development team to build the product and reviewing performance after launch.
* **Working with the Scrum team:** Attending daily meetings and sprint planning.
* **Acceptance testing (UAT):** Making sure the engineering team’s output matches the user’s need.
* **Analyzing metrics:** Reviewing post-launch data and planning later improvements.

## A vital distinction: product manager (PM) versus project manager

One of the most common mistakes in organizations is mixing up the product manager and the project manager. Although the two roles overlap, their focus is completely different.

| Trait | Product manager | Project manager |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary focus** | **What** and **why** | **When** and **how** |
| **Responsibility** | Product success in the market and user satisfaction | Completing the project on time and on budget |
| **Mindset** | Outcome-oriented | Output-oriented |
| **Key question** | Are we building the right product? | Are we building the product right? |
| **End of the work** | Never-ending (the product lifecycle) | Ends when the project is delivered |

> **Example:** The product manager decides that, based on market need, an “online chat” capability should be added to the app (strategy). The project manager plans how the engineering team will implement that capability in two weeks with the available resources (execution).

## Why organizations need product management (the added value)

You might ask why the CEO or the engineering team cannot do this work. A professional product manager removes several large risks:

**1. Preventing “useless features”:**
Many companies fall into the *build trap*: they keep adding features without knowing whether anyone uses them. The product manager stops resources being wasted on unnecessary features.

**2. Filling the communication gap (breaking silos):**
In large organizations, marketing does not know what engineering is doing, and sales promises things the product team cannot build. The product manager acts as “glue” and aligns every unit around a shared goal (the product vision).

**3. Speeding up learning:**
Modern product management is based on learning fast. Instead of spending six months building a complete product, the product manager ships early versions (MVP), gets feedback quickly, and corrects the path. That means a lower risk of final failure.

## Types of product manager (specialization in the role)

As product management has matured, more specialist branches have appeared:

* **Technical PM:** Focus on infrastructure products, APIs, and developer tools.
* **Growth PM:** Focus on user acquisition, conversion, and revenue (usually with rapid A/B experiments).
* **Data PM:** Focus on products based on artificial intelligence and big data.
* **Platform PM:** Focus on internal systems that other product teams use.

## Conclusion

Product management is more than a job; it is a **way of thinking**—a way of thinking in which “solving the user’s problem” comes before “technical solutions,” and “business results” come before “the number of features produced.”

A successful product manager is not someone who has all the answers, but someone who asks the right questions and leads the team to find the answers. In later parts of these docs, we will go into operational detail and learn how to apply these principles in the real world.
